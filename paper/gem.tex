\begin{center}\textbf{Abstract}\end{center}
\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{adjustwidth}{1.1cm}{1.1cm}
\noindent
This paper examines Global Equities Momentum (GEM), the dual momentum rule
proposed by \citet{antonacci2016}, and asks where its returns actually come
from. The rule combines an absolute filter, which decides whether to hold
equities at all by comparing US equity with Treasury bills, and a relative
filter, which then selects between US and non-US equities. We replicate it on
monthly total-return data from January 1971 to July 2026, a period spanning
four equity bear markets of more than 30\%, a complete cycle of rising and
falling interest rates, and sixteen years of sustained US outperformance. Our
findings reveal that \$100 invested in GEM would have grown to \$258{,}057,
against \$37{,}908 for a passive holding of the S\&P 500 --- a compound annual
return of 15.18\% against 11.27\%, with a maximum drawdown of 21.7\% against
51.0\% and a Sharpe ratio of 0.83 against 0.50, at an average of 1.5 trades a
year. Decomposing that result, we find the two momentum filters to be
complements rather than substitutes: run separately they add 77 and 203 basis
points a year, run together 391. We further find that none of the excess return
comes from diversification. A static portfolio holding GEM's own average
allocation returns 10.03\% a year, below the index, so the asset basket is a
handicap that the timing must first overcome; diversification does, however,
supply 9 of the 29 percentage points of drawdown reduction. Although the record
over the full sample is strong, we do not read it as evidence that the rule
will continue to work: GEM has underperformed a passive index by 4.8 points a
year since 2010, and its deepest drawdown in fifty-five years occurred in
2021--2023, while it was holding its defensive asset.

\section{Introduction}

Dual momentum is among the best known tactical asset allocation rules available
to a private investor. \citet{antonacci2016} proposes combining two forms of
momentum --- a relative test that picks the stronger of two equity markets, and
an absolute test that decides whether to hold equities at all --- and reports
that the combination beats a passive index by a wide margin, with far smaller
drawdowns. The rule is simple enough to run in a spreadsheet, trades roughly
once or twice a year, and has been widely adopted.

The strategy's performance has been documented extensively, by its author and
by others. This paper asks a narrower question: \emph{which part of the rule
produces that performance?} GEM combines two momentum filters and moves between
three assets, and those components can be separated and priced individually. We
do so, and find that the answer is not the one a reader would naturally assume.

We replicate the strategy over 1971--2026 and reproduce the headline result
closely: 15.18\% a year against 11.27\% for the S\&P 500, a maximum drawdown of
21.7\% against 51.0\%, a Sharpe ratio of 0.83 against 0.50, and an annualised
alpha of 6.61\% with a Newey--West $t$-statistic of 4.31. We then decompose it,
and two findings emerge.

First, \textbf{the two momentum filters are complements rather than
substitutes}, and the size of the interaction is larger than is generally
appreciated. Run on their own, absolute momentum adds 77 basis points a year
and relative momentum 203; run together they add 391. The 111 basis points that
neither half earns alone come from the absolute filter removing the obligation
to choose between two bad options.

Second, and less intuitively, \textbf{none of the excess return comes from
diversification}. A static portfolio holding GEM's own average allocation ---
46\% US equity, 28\% non-US, 25\% bonds --- returned 10.03\% a year, below the
S\&P 500. Over this sample the basket of assets was a handicap of 124 basis
points; the switching had to overcome it before adding anything. Risk splits
differently: diversification supplies 9 of the 29 percentage points of drawdown
reduction, and timing the other 20.

The paper also reports what the replication shows about the strategy's recent
record. GEM has underperformed a passive index by 4.8 points a year since 2010,
and its deepest drawdown in fifty-five years occurred in 2021--2023 --- while
it was sitting in its defensive asset. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} sets out
the mechanism and what can and cannot be concluded from it.

One methodological choice is worth flagging at the outset. Antonacci's own
backtest rests on Global Financial Data, MSCI, Bloomberg Barclays and Ibbotson
series, all of which require paid subscriptions, so his figures cannot be
checked without a data budget. We rebuild the series from free sources and
publish the dataset alongside the code, which makes every number here open to
contradiction. Section~\ref{sec:data} sets out what that costs in coverage.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} sets out the rule and
what each of its two filters is for. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data,
its provenance and its discontinuities, and the implementation.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Section~\ref{sec:robustness}
tests them against the one free parameter and against trading costs, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations.

\section{The Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}

Momentum is the tendency of an asset that has performed well to go on
performing well. It comes in two forms that are often conflated but that answer
different questions, and Global Equities Momentum is built on the distinction
between them.

\textbf{Relative momentum} is cross-sectional. It ranks assets against one
another and holds the winners. This is the effect documented by
\citet{jegadeesh1993} for individual US stocks, and since found across
industries, countries, asset classes and centuries. It answers the question
\emph{which asset?} and, by construction, it is always invested in something:
ranking cannot tell you that everything on the list is a bad idea.

\textbf{Absolute momentum} --- also called time-series momentum
\citep{moskowitz2012} --- compares an asset with its own past rather than with
its peers. If the trailing return exceeds the return on cash, the trend is up
and the asset is held; otherwise it is not. This answers a different question,
\emph{should we be invested at all?}, and it is the only one of the two that
can take risk off the table.

\citet{antonacci2016} combines the two into a single rule he calls Global
Equities Momentum. The insight is that the two filters are complements rather
than substitutes, and that the order in which they are applied matters. The
absolute test acts as a gate: only when equities are trending up does the
relative test get to choose between them. Figure~\ref{fig:rule} states the rule
in full.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{00_decision_rule.pdf}
  \caption{The Global Equities Momentum decision rule. Absolute momentum
  decides whether to hold equities at all; relative momentum decides which.
  Percentages give the share of months spent in each branch over 1971--2026.}
  \label{fig:rule}
\end{figure}

The strategy uses four building blocks: US equity, non-US equity, US aggregate
bonds and Treasury bills. Equities are the assets to be held; bonds are the
refuge when the absolute test fails; T-bills are not held at all but serve as
the hurdle rate that the absolute test measures against. The lookback for both
tests is twelve months, and the rule is evaluated once a month.

Two design choices in that rule deserve to be stated explicitly, because they
are what separate GEM from a generic trend-following system.

The first is that \textbf{the absolute test is applied to US equity alone}, not
to whichever equity market the relative test would have chosen. The US market
serves as the bellwether for global equity risk. This is a simplification, and
it means the strategy can hold ex-US equity in a month when ex-US equity is
itself falling, provided the US market is not.

The second is that \textbf{the escape asset is aggregate bonds, not cash}, even
though the hurdle is measured against cash. When equities fail the absolute
test, the strategy does not sit in T-bills; it takes duration and credit risk
instead. Over the sample this choice adds return, but it also means the
defensive position is not risk-free: it lost value in 2022, when bonds and
equities fell together.

\section{Data and Methodology}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The data problem}

Replicating GEM requires four monthly total-return series reaching back far
enough to include more than one full market cycle. \citet{antonacci2018} builds
his from the S\&P 500, MSCI indices for the non-US leg, Global Financial Data
before MSCI begins, the Bloomberg Barclays US Aggregate for bonds, and Ibbotson
series before the Aggregate exists. Every one of those is a paid subscription.
A reader without a data budget --- which is to say most readers --- cannot check
the result.

This paper imposes the opposite constraint: \textbf{every series is built from
free, publicly accessible sources}. That is a restriction, and it costs
something in coverage, as set out below. But it buys the property that matters
most for a replication: anyone can rebuild the dataset, or verify the one we
publish, at no cost.

\subsection{The four series}

The dataset holds four monthly total-return index levels in US dollars, from
December 1969 to July 2026 --- 680 observations. Table~\ref{tab:provenance}
records where each one comes from, and Figure~\ref{fig:provenance} shows the
same information as a timeline.

The historical core, running to December 2016, comes from a public CSV
redistributed on GitHub that reproduces Antonacci's own construction. It is
extended to the present with Yahoo Finance, MSCI's public index endpoint and
the \citet{frenchdata} library. The splice is a change of base only: the live
series is multiplied by a constant so that it coincides with the core in the
junction month, which leaves every monthly return on both sides untouched.

Three features of Table~\ref{tab:provenance} matter for how the results should
be read, and none of them is an artefact of using free data --- Antonacci's own
series have the same breaks.

\textbf{The non-US leg is spliced in 1988, by design.} The objective is to
measure the non-US equity market in full, emerging markets included, since that
is the opportunity set an investor allocating outside the United States
actually faces. No index covering emerging markets existed before 1988, so the
earlier months necessarily use MSCI World ex USA, which is developed markets
only. MSCI created its Emerging Markets index in 1988 and launched the All
Country World family in 1990 with history back-calculated to December 1987 ---
which is precisely where the series switches. From that month the leg uses MSCI
ACWI ex USA, the broadest measure available.

The splice therefore reflects what could be measured at each date rather than a
compromise. It also costs very little: at inception the emerging-market
constituents represented less than 1\% of the global opportunity set, so in
1988 the two indices were measuring nearly the same thing and the join is
close to seamless in return terms. What changes afterwards is the trajectory,
not the join --- the emerging-market weight grows steadily, to roughly a
quarter of the ex-US index today. The consequence is for interpreting the later
decades, not for the validity of the splice.

\textbf{The bond leg changes nature in 1976.} The Bloomberg Barclays US
Aggregate does not exist before January 1976. Prior months use a monthly
rebalanced blend of 40\% intermediate Treasuries and 60\% intermediate
corporates. The Aggregate includes securitised debt and carries a different
duration, so the pre-1976 and post-1976 halves of that leg are related but not
identical.

\textbf{The US leg changes provider but not index.} The Ibbotson large-cap
series and the S\&P 500 total return series measure the same thing; the 2013
handover has no economic content.

Table~\ref{tab:summary} gives the statistical properties of the four inputs
before any strategy is applied to them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Summary statistics of the four input series.}
  \label{tab:summary}
  \input{tables/t2_summary}
\end{table}

Two things in that table shape everything that follows. US equity outperformed
non-US equity by 1.6 percentage points a year over the sample while being
\emph{less} volatile, which means the strategy's non-US leg starts at a
disadvantage. And both equity legs lost more than half their value at their
worst, against 17\% for bonds, which is the drawdown the absolute momentum
filter exists to avoid.

\subsection{Verification}

Because the historical core is a redistributed file rather than a primary
source, each of the four series was checked against independent providers over
every month they have in common: nine comparisons in all, with correlations
between 0.96 and 0.9999. The audit found one genuine defect --- three
observations in the T-bill leg had lost their leading digit --- which is
corrected before use. Appendix~\ref{app:audit} gives the full apparatus and the
repair.

\subsection{Implementation}

At the close of month $t$ the trailing twelve-month total return is computed for
US equity, non-US equity and T-bills. The rule of Figure~\ref{fig:rule} selects
one asset, and that position earns the return of month $t+1$. Formally, with
$P_{i,t}$ the level of series $i$ at month-end $t$, the signal is
\[
  R_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-12}} - 1 ,
\]
and the asset chosen on $R_{\cdot,t}$ is held through month $t+1$. No quantity
dated $t+1$ enters the decision, so the backtest is free of look-ahead bias by
construction rather than by inspection. The first signal uses December 1970, so
the results begin in January 1971 and run for 667 months.

The strategy is compared with four benchmarks: the S\&P 500 alone; non-US
equity alone; a 60/40 portfolio; and a static mix carrying GEM's own average
allocation, rebalanced monthly. That last benchmark is the one that separates
what the strategy earns from holding a diversified basket from what it earns by
switching between its parts, and Section~\ref{sec:results} uses it for exactly
that.

Monthly returns from a momentum rule are autocorrelated, because the rule holds
the same asset for months at a time. An ordinary $t$-test would therefore
overstate significance. All $t$-statistics reported here use
\citet{newey1987} standard errors with a Bartlett kernel and the automatic
bandwidth $4(T/100)^{2/9}$. Transaction costs are set to zero in the headline
results and varied in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}; taxes are not modelled.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Headline performance}

Over the full sample GEM compounded at 15.18\% a year against 11.27\% for the
S\&P 500, with lower volatility (12.91\% against 15.20\%) and less than half
the worst drawdown (21.7\% against 51.0\%). Figure~\ref{fig:equity} shows what
a difference of 3.9 points a year does when it is compounded over fifty-five
years: \$100 invested in GEM in January 1971 becomes \$258{,}057 by July 2026,
against \$37{,}908 for the same \$100 left in the index --- close to seven
times as much, from a strategy that traded 1.5 times a year on average.
Table~\ref{tab:performance} gives the full comparison.

GEM's drawdowns are not simply smaller versions of the index's; they are

Two features of Table~\ref{tab:performance} are worth pausing on. The
improvement is concentrated in the left tail: GEM's worst month is $-14.5$\%
against $-21.5$\% for the index, and its fifth-percentile monthly return is
$-4.8$\% against $-6.4$\%. That is what an absolute momentum filter is for --- it
does not make good months better, it makes bad months rarer and shallower. And
the strategy is positive in 67\% of months against 63\% for the index, a small
edge in frequency that compounds precisely because the losing months are so
much less severe.

\subsection{Where the return comes from}

The rule has two filters, and they can be run separately.
Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} does exactly that: absolute momentum alone holds
US equity when it beats T-bills and bonds otherwise, never touching the non-US
leg; relative momentum alone always holds the stronger of the two equity
markets, with no escape to bonds.

Each half beats the index, and each does so in a characteristic way. Absolute
momentum adds 77 basis points a year, but it cuts the drawdown from 51\% to
30\% and the volatility from 15.2\% to 12.3\% --- it is a risk filter that
happens to pay for itself. Relative momentum adds 203 basis points, three times
as much return, while leaving the drawdown at 55\%, slightly \emph{worse} than
the index: always invested in equities, it rode 2008 down like everything else.

Together they add 391 basis points. That is the result worth stating carefully,
because $77 + 203 = 280$, not 391. \textbf{The two filters are not additive.}
The combination earns 111 basis points a year that neither half earns alone.

The mechanism is visible in what the filters do to each other. Relative
momentum's weakness is that ranking always returns an answer: in 2008 it had to
choose between two collapsing markets. The absolute filter removes that
obligation, so the relative choice is only exercised in the states where being
in equities is worth something at all. Conversely, absolute momentum's weakness
is that its escape adds little return; giving it a second equity market to
choose from when the gate is open is what turns a defensive overlay into a
strategy. This complementarity is \citeauthor{antonacci2016}'s central claim,
and it reproduces here on a different sample and different data.

\subsection{Allocation or timing?}

A strategy that moves between three assets earns two different things: whatever
comes from holding that basket at all, and whatever comes from choosing when to
hold which part of it. Comparing GEM with the S\&P 500 alone confounds the two,
because the index is a single asset and GEM is three.

Table~\ref{tab:timing} separates them with a benchmark carrying GEM's own
average allocation --- 46\% US equity, 28\% non-US, 25\% bonds --- held fixed
and rebalanced monthly. The weights are read off the backtest, not chosen: they
are what the rule actually did, with the timing removed.

The result is not what a diversification argument would predict. That static
mix returned 10.03\% a year, \emph{below} the S\&P 500's 11.27\%. Over this
sample the basket was a handicap worth 124 basis points a year, for the reason
Table~\ref{tab:summary} already showed: non-US equity underperformed US equity
while being more volatile, and bonds returned less than either.

So the decomposition is stark. The switching is worth 515 basis points a year
gross, of which 124 go to cancelling the drag of the basket, leaving the 391
observed. \textbf{None of GEM's excess return over the S\&P 500 comes from
diversification; all of it comes from timing, which must first pay off a
handicap.} A reader who takes GEM to be a well-diversified portfolio with a
momentum overlay has the causality backwards.

Risk splits the other way. The static mix cuts the maximum drawdown from 51.0\%
to 41.9\%, and the timing takes it from there to 21.7\%. Diversification
therefore does its conventional job on risk --- 9.0 points of it --- while
costing return; the switching supplies both the remaining 20.3 points of
drawdown reduction and the entire return advantage.

\subsection{Risk-adjusted performance}

Regressing GEM's monthly excess return on the S\&P 500's, over the 667 months
of the sample, gives
\[
  r_{\text{GEM},t} - r_{f,t}
  \;=\; \alpha + \beta \,(r_{\text{S\&P},t} - r_{f,t}) + \varepsilon_t ,
\]
with an annualised $\alpha$ of 6.61\% ($t = 4.31$), $\beta = 0.57$ and
$R^2 = 0.45$. Standard errors are Newey--West; an ordinary $t$-test would read
higher, because a rule that holds the same asset for months at a time produces
autocorrelated residuals.

A beta of 0.57 is the quantitative form of what Figure~\ref{fig:allocation}
shows: the strategy is out of US equity slightly more than half the time. The
$R^2$ of 0.45 says that most of GEM's variance is \emph{not} the index's
variance --- which is what one would want from a strategy whose defensive
position is a different asset class.

The $t$-statistic of 4.31 clears the threshold of three that
\citet{harvey2016} propose for claims discovered after extensive data mining.
That is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, and Section~\ref{sec:robustness}
tests how much of it survives perturbation of the one free parameter.

\subsection{Behaviour through time}

The aggregate numbers conceal a strong pattern. Figure~\ref{fig:annual} plots
calendar-year returns side by side, and the asymmetry is the whole story: GEM
rarely beats the index in a good year, and rarely loses much in a bad one. In
the eleven calendar years the S\&P 500 declined, it averaged $-14.4$\% while
GEM averaged $-0.4$\%. \textbf{The strategy earns its record by not
participating in disasters, not by outperforming in booms.}

Figure~\ref{fig:allocation} shows what it held to achieve that, and
Figure~\ref{fig:rolling} shows the consistency of the result. Over rolling
three-year windows GEM is ahead of the index 59\% of the time --- a majority,
but not an overwhelming one, and the losing stretches are long enough to be
uncomfortable for anyone actually holding the strategy.

Table~\ref{tab:decades} makes the concentration explicit. GEM beat the index in
every decade from the 1970s through the 2000s, by margins of 1.3 to 11.4 points
a year. It has lost in both decades since: $-4.5$ points a year in the 2010s
and $-5.4$ in the 2020s so far. Every result reported above is therefore an
average over a sample in which the strategy worked for four decades and then
stopped. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} takes that up directly.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

GEM has one free parameter, the lookback window, and one cost the backtest
ignores. Both deserve to be pushed on, because a result that survives only at
its chosen setting is not a result.

\subsection{The lookback window}

Figure~\ref{fig:lookback} and Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:robustness} vary the
window from three to twenty-four months. Across that range the CAGR runs from
12.21\% to 15.18\%, an amplitude of 2.97 points, and every setting beats the
S\&P 500's 11.27\%. The strategy is therefore not fragile in the sense of
working at one setting and failing at the others.

But the twelve-month window is \textbf{the maximum of the grid}, and that
should be said plainly rather than buried. Antonacci's chosen parameter is the
best-performing one out of twenty-two tested here. Some of that is real ---
returns rise fairly smoothly from three to twelve months and fall away after,
which is the shape one expects from a genuine effect rather than from noise ---
but a peak is still a peak, and an out-of-sample user should expect the middle
of the range, near 13.5\%, rather than the top.

Maximum drawdown is the less comfortable dimension. It ranges from $-19.6$\% to
$-28.3$\%, and its variation across windows looks considerably more like noise
than the return does: adjacent settings differ by six points with no pattern.
The risk reduction GEM delivers is real but its magnitude is not precisely
estimated.

\subsection{Transaction costs}

The rule changes asset 1.49 times a year on average, which is very low
turnover; the absolute filter is what keeps it there, since a rule that
switched on price alone would trade far more. Panel~B charges a cost on every
change. At 25 basis points the CAGR falls from 15.18\% to 14.76\%, and even at
100 basis points --- far above anything an ETF investor would pay today ---
13.51\% remains well clear of the index.

Costs are therefore not the binding constraint on this strategy. Taxes may
be: the rule realises gains at unpredictable intervals, and in a taxable
account the drag would be materially larger than anything in Panel~B. We do not
model it.

\section{Limitations and Discussion}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{The strategy has not worked since 2009}

Table~\ref{tab:decades} showed the pattern; it is worth quantifying. Over
1971--2009 GEM returned 17.71\% a year against 10.03\% for the S\&P 500, an
advantage of 7.7 points. Over 2010--2026 it returned 9.42\% against 14.26\%, a
shortfall of 4.8 points. Sixteen years is not a blip.

The cause is specific and measurable. Since 2010 the rule has been out of US
equity in only 26\% of months --- 21 in bonds and 31 in non-US equity --- so
the shortfall cannot be explained by excessive caution. It comes from
\emph{when} those months fell. During the 21 defensive months since 2010 the
S\&P 500 returned 34.1\% annualised. During the 149 defensive months of
1971--2009 it returned 5.3\%.

That inversion is the whole result. A twelve-month rule exits after a decline
has already happened and re-enters after a recovery is already underway. When
bear markets were long --- 1973--74, 2000--02, 2007--09 --- the delay was
affordable and the exit was right. Since 2010 the declines have been sharp and
V-shaped: 2011, 2018, 2020 and 2022 all round-tripped faster than the signal
could follow. Roughly three quarters of the shortfall comes from these bond
whipsaws and the remaining quarter from holding non-US equity through a period
of sustained US outperformance.

Whether this is regime-dependence that will reverse, or the post-publication
decay that \citet{mclean2016} document across anomalies generally, cannot be
settled with one strategy and sixteen years. Both readings are consistent with
the evidence. What can be said is that the strategy's published record was
established almost entirely before it was published.

\subsection{The defensive asset is not safe}

Section~\ref{sec:strategy} noted that GEM escapes into aggregate bonds rather
than cash. The cost of that choice is concentrated in one episode, and it is
the worst in the sample.

\textbf{GEM's deepest drawdown over fifty-five years, $-21.7$\%, runs from
December 2021 to October 2023} --- and the strategy spent 13 of those 23 months
in bonds. In 2022 it was defensive for seven months of twelve, and lost 16.9\%
on the year while bonds lost 13.0\% and the S\&P 500 lost 18.1\%. The rule
worked as designed, moved to safety as intended, and the safety lost money.

This matters beyond one bad year. Every drawdown statistic in this paper is
computed on a sample in which bonds rallied for four decades. A strategy whose
risk control depends on bonds rising when equities fall has been tested almost
entirely in the period when that correlation held.

\subsection{Data and specification caveats}

\textbf{The series are not fully homogeneous.} As Table~\ref{tab:provenance}
sets out, the non-US leg is spliced in 1988 and the bond leg in 1976. Neither
join is itself material --- emerging markets were under 1\% of the global
opportunity set in 1988 --- but the emerging-market weight grows afterwards, so
the later decades measure a somewhat broader asset than the earlier ones.

\textbf{The historical core is a redistribution.} It is audited against nine
independent references in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}, and one real defect was
found and repaired, which is evidence the audit works rather than evidence the
file is sound. A reader who requires primary sources throughout would have to
restrict the sample to 1997 onwards --- losing 1973--74 and 2000--02, which is
to say losing the episodes where the strategy demonstrates its value.

\textbf{The parameter was not chosen by us.} We inherited the twelve-month
window from \citet{antonacci2016}, which avoids in-sample optimisation on our
part but does not undo whatever selection went into the original choice.

\textbf{Taxes are not modelled}, and neither is the practical difficulty of
holding a strategy through the 2010s while it underperformed a passive index
every year.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Over 1971--2026 Global Equities Momentum returned 15.18\% a year against
11.27\% for the S\&P 500, with a maximum drawdown of 21.7\% against 51.0\% and
an annualised alpha of 6.61\% ($t = 4.31$). The replication is close to
Antonacci's own figures.

Two things about \emph{how} the strategy produces that record are less widely
appreciated than the record itself.

The two momentum filters are complements, not substitutes. Run separately they
add 77 and 203 basis points a year; run together they add 391. The absolute
filter is what makes the relative choice worth making, because it removes the
obligation to choose in the states where no choice is good.

And the excess return is entirely timing. A static portfolio holding GEM's own
average allocation returned 10.03\% a year, below the index: the basket of
assets was a handicap over this sample, not an advantage. Diversification did
reduce risk --- 9 of the 29 points of drawdown reduction --- but every basis
point of return advantage came from switching.

The strategy has nonetheless failed to beat a passive index since 2009, by 4.8
points a year, and its worst drawdown in fifty-five years occurred in
2021--2023, in its defensive asset. Both facts are consistent with a rule that
was calibrated on an era of long bear markets and falling bond yields, and that
has since met neither. Whether that is regime dependence or decay is not
something this sample can decide.

The dataset, the code and every exhibit are published, so any of the above can
be checked, contradicted or extended.

\section{Independent Verification of the Dataset}
\label{app:audit}

The historical core of the dataset is a CSV redistributed on GitHub that
reproduces Antonacci's construction. It is convenient and it reaches back to
1969, but it is not a primary source, so it cannot simply be trusted.

Each of the four series was therefore rebuilt from providers with no connection
to that file, and the two were compared over every month they have in common.
Table~\ref{tab:audit} reports the nine comparisons. Correlations of monthly
returns run from 0.9611 to 0.9999 and annualised differences in return from
$-0.04$ to $+1.16$ percentage points. The larger gaps are expected rather than
alarming: the MSCI and EAFE references measure a narrower universe than the
audited series, and the ETF references are net of fees.

Three further checks were run.

\textbf{Timing.} A redistributed file shifted by one month would look correct
on a chart but would destroy a momentum backtest, since the signal would be
built on returns the strategy could not yet have observed. Correlations were
therefore computed at lags $-2$ to $+2$. Every reference peaks at lag zero,
with one documented exception: FRED's three-month bill series is a monthly
average of a \emph{yield}, and the yield quoted in month $t$ is earned over
month $t+1$, so its peak at lag $+1$ is the correct economic relationship. The
strictly comparable reference for the cash leg, Kenneth French's one-month
bill, peaks at lag zero with a correlation of 0.9938.

\textbf{Splice joints.} A badly made splice usually shows up as one freak month
at the junction. Each junction month was scored against the distribution of its
own series; all four fall within three standard deviations.

\textbf{Structure.} The panel was checked for a continuous monthly index, no
duplicate months, strictly positive levels, and no monthly return beyond 40\%
in absolute value.

\subsection*{A defect found, and repaired}

The structural check failed on the file as published. A cash total-return index
accrues a positive yield every month, so any month-on-month \emph{fall} is by
construction a data error rather than a market movement. Three such falls
appear, all in November: 1990, 1991 and 1992. In each the level has lost its
leading digit --- 0.366 where the surrounding months require 10.366, 0.970
where they require 10.970, 1.366 where they require 11.366.

Left uncorrected this injects a monthly return of $-96$\% followed by
$+2{,}749$\% into the cash leg, which corrupts the absolute momentum signal for
the twelve months following each occurrence. Each bad point is replaced by the
geometric mean of its two neighbours, which recovers the dropped digit to four
significant figures without hard-coding any constant. After the repair, the
correlation of the cash leg with Kenneth French's one-month bill rises from
0.025 to 0.9938 and the tracking error falls from 442\% to 0.11\%.

A second, smaller discrepancy was handled by substitution rather than repair.
Over 2013--2016 the file's cash column implies 0.95\% a year when the actual
Treasury bill rate was 0.06\%. Kenneth French's series matches the file to 0.00
percentage points a year over 1970--2012 and is correct where the file is not,
so it is used for the whole sample. This removes both a splice and a defect. Its
effect on the strategy is nil: no allocation decision changes in any of the 667
months.
